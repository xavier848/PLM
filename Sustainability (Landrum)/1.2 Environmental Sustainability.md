**Overview**

Environmental sustainability is the most important component of sustainability; it contains within it both social and economic sustainability. Environmental sustainability refers to the protection of our planet, our life supporting system. More than half of global GDP (approximately USD 44 trillion) depends upon nature and its services, yet governments and companies overlook this value (Herweijer et al., 2020). Industries or economic sectors that are the most reliant on nature include construction, agriculture, and food and beverages and nearly all other industries depend on these 3 sectors (Herweijer et al., 2020). Remember that without a habitable planet, there will be no social or economic systems.

Let's look more closely at what is included when we refer to environmental sustainability.

**Planetary Boundaries**

Scientists have identified 9 **planetary boundaries**  needed for survival on Earth (Rockstrom et al., 2009).

1. *Stratospheric ozone depletion*  - refers to the protection of the ozone layer that filters out ultraviolet radiation form the sun.
2. *Biosphere integrity*  - refers to changes in ecosystems due to human activities that are destroying ecosystems, biodiversity, and leading to extinctions of plant and animal life.
3. *Novel entities*  - refers to the presence of toxic chemical pollutants in the environment.
4. *Climate change*  - refers to the accumulation of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, particularly carbon dioxide, that are leading to a warming global climate.
5. *Ocean acidification*  - refers to the carbon dioxide that is accumulating in oceans that transforms into carbonic acid and destroys ocean life, such as corals, shellfish, and plankton.
6. *Freshwater change*  - refers to changes in the freshwater cycle caused by human activities, such as land system change, that leads to water scarcity.
7. *Land system change*  - refers to the conversion of forests, grasslands, wetlands, and other vegetation types into agricultural land that leads to biodiversity loss, changes in water flows and biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and others), and climate change through the loss of carbon sinks (organic matter, such as tress, that draws carbon from the atmosphere and stores it).
8. *Biogeochemical flows*  - refers to the cycles of nitrogen and phosphorus that have been radically altered due to industrial and agricultural processes.
9. *Atmospheric aerosol loading*  - refers to the interaction between aerosols emitted by human activity and the earth's hydrological cycle. While further work is needed to quantify boundaries and current atmospheric aerosol pollution, this interaction could impact water cycles, biodiversity, and climate change.

The scientific research reveals that we have already breached the safe limits for six of the nine boundaries, an additional boundary is still under study, and we are only safely operating within two of these life support boundaries - stratospheric ozone depletion and ocean acidification (Rockstrom et al., 2009; Steffen et al., 2015; Stockholm Resilience Center, 2022).

**Climate**

Perhaps the most pressing planetary issue of our time is the climate crisis. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is an international nonpartisan working group formed in 1988. Since 1990, the IPCC has issued six of the world's most comprehensive reports and recommendations on **climate change** . The IPCC warns us that we must contain average global warming within 1.5° Celsius (2.7° Fahrenheit), however, due to our failure to make critical and significant change, the IPCC reports that we are currently on track for a 3.2°C rise in average global temperature (IPCC, 2022), well beyond the 1.5°C recommendation. If we do not address the climate crisis, we will lose approximately 18% of global GDP (Herweijer et al., 2020).

Note that weather is not the same as climate. Weather constitutes the daily fluctuations and changes in temperature and precipitation whereas climate constitutes long-term patterns and trends in weather.

How do we know historical temperatures to compare them to current temperatures?

- We have taken actual measurements for approximately 150 years (as seen in the NASA video above).
- We use a “proxy” (our best scientific estimates) for measurements more than 150 years ago. Some of the proxies used include tree rings, ice cores, coral reef layers, lake sediment, etc.

Because the climate is warming,

- oceans are warming and causing acidification
- sea levels are rising
- growing seasons are longer
- the number of frost days per year have decreased
- spring events arrive earlier
- plant and animal species are migrating further north or to higher altitudes
- differences between daytime and nighttime temperatures are decreasing
- heatwaves are longer and more frequent
- intensity of hurricanes is increasing
- intensity of rainfall is increasing in some regions while drought in increasing in other regions
- permafrost (permanently frozen soil) is melting
- glaciers and sea ice are shrinking

Increasing average global temperature has one cause: excess greenhouse gasses in the atmosphere. Earth's heating and cooling cycles occur approximately every 100,000 years, but due to human impact primarily from burning fossil fuels, these cycles have occurred in approximately 50 years due to a buildup of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Even though carbon dioxide is a building block for plant life, the rate of carbon dioxide buildup in the atmosphere has risen at a rapid rate over the past 175 years with a sharp uptick over the past 30-70 years; this overabundance is now killing life on Earth (equivalent to the fact that plants need water to survive, but overwatering will also kill plants).

It is estimated that 60% of the world's greenhouse gas emissions are caused by the energy, transport, buildings, and water infrastructure sectors. The IPCC reports that rapid and deep transitions in these industries are required to limit average global warming to 1.5 C degrees. The OECD estimates this will require 6.9 trillion USD investments annually through 2030. Government budgets are insufficient. Private capital is necessary (Macquarie et al., 2020).

Reducing carbon emissions is the #1 sustainability goal of companies. But how to reduce a company's carbon emissions is unique to each company, depending upon a company's greenhouse gas emissions inventory results (aka carbon footprint). Regardless of the various initiatives used to reduce carbon emissions, it is imperative that companies align carbon reduction goals with climate science instead of setting arbitrary goals. The [Science-Based Targets initiative](https://sciencebasedtargets.org/) (SBTi) assists companies in developing science-based carbon reduction goals. We will discuss more about the SBTi later.

In the following sections, we will examine some of the specific human activities that are leading to excess greenhouse gasses and global warming.

**Energy**

***Fossil Fuels***

Fossil fuels are dug from the earth to be burned for energy. Types of fossil fuels include:

- Oil/petroleum - fossilized terrestrial marine plankton
- Natural gas - fossilized terrestrial marine plankton
- Coal - fossilized terrestrial plant material

Fossil fuels are extracted from the earth's surface in tar sands, oil shales, land-based oil fields, and marine deposits.

Many of the products you buy and use today are also made from fossil fuels.

To reduce fossil fuel usage and the creation of greenhouse gas emissions, we begin with energy efficiency improvements.

- Conduct an energy audit to determine current energy usage.
- Set measurable and attainable goals for energy reduction.
- Develop and implement tactics to achieve the goals.
- Measure progress and evaluate the outcomes; take corrective action, as needed.

Water covers more than 70% of the surface of Earth, but 97.5% is saltwater and only 2.5% is freshwater (mostly in the form of frozen ice and snow).

Overpopulation, overconsumption, and uneven distribution is leading to water scarcity; we are unable to meet the demand for water.

***Water Pollution***

Rivers are increasingly polluted by fertilizers, pesticides, chemical wastes, and industrial spills and waterways are common dumping sites.

- Toxins from agriculture enter waterways and the food chain. They accumulate at the top of the food chain and cause health problems in the animal and humans that consume the animal.
  - Toxins have been found in fish throughout the world.
  - Fish can become deformed through exposure to toxins.
- Cruise ships routinely dump untreated waste into the ocean; although they are not required to report the amount dumped, it is estimated that cruise ships dump more than a billion gallons of sewage in the ocean each year (Friends of the Earth, 2013).
- 90% of human waste is discarded into rivers in emerging economies
- Lack of access to clean water is the largest cause of disease transmission and poverty.

***Water & Climate***

Climate change results in too much water in wet areas (flooding) and not enough water in dry areas (drought). Climate change also leads to ocean acidification and rising sea levels. These climate change impacts damage ecosystems and further stress water sensitive areas.

**Waste**

Every discarded item carries large quantities of embedded energy, water, and material resources that were used to make the product. There are numerous waste streams:

- Municipal solid waste - collected from households, businesses/offices
- Medical waste - from medical facilities, including animal medical facilities
- Industrial waste - from manufacturing and other industrial processes (includes chemicals, pigments, metals, ash, paint, radioactive waste, sludge, etc.)
- Household hazardous waste - includes cleaners, automotive oil, household chemicals, nail polish, paint, fuel, aerosol cans, insect and rodent poisons, etc.
- Electronic waste - discarded electronics and electronics components
- Construction & demolition waste - includes plumbing, drywall, wood, bricks, insulation, roofing, etc. from construction sites

Many countries collect data on municipal solid waste, but no data is kept on industrial waste. In the U.S., it is estimated that

- 93% of national trash is industrial waste (manufacturing and other industrial processes),
- 3% is municipal waste (household and office/business), and
- 3% comes from other sources (EPA, 2003)

In the EU, it is estimated that

- 10% of total waste is municipal waste and
- 86% of EU-wide trash is industrial waste (Eurostat, 2022).

In many countries, the construction industry produces the most waste following by mining, agriculture, e-waste, and medical waste.

Worldwide, it is estimated that less than 20% of municipal solid waste is recycled (Tiseo, 2022). How much industrial waste is recycled? No one knows. But the size of the problem is obvious.

The Waste Management Hierarchy (EPA, 2022) ranks waste management strategies from most preferred (most environmentally-friendly) to least preferred (least environmentally-friendly).

**Electronic waste**  (e-waste) is another concern. Some e-waste is buried or incinerated, which releases toxins into the air, land, and water. In developed economies, much of the e-waste is shipped to emerging economies where individuals extract precious metals from the electronics, often without training or safety equipment to handle toxic substances. Laws have been passed in the Americas and Europe to prevent shipment of e-waste to emerging economies, but there is often no tracking or accountability and the waste gets shipped abroad anyway. Agbogbloshie (Accra, Ghana) is often referred to as “the world's largest e-waste dump” but it is so large, in fact, that it is also described as a city within a city. To learn more about Agbogbloshie, see a brief photographic exhibit [here](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/gallery/2014/feb/27/agbogbloshie-worlds-largest-e-waste-dump-in-pictures) (optional).

**Plastic waste**  is of particular concern. Even though many plastics can be recycled, the majority of plastic is used a single time and is not recycled, primarily because governments do have have the recycling infrastructure in place. Globally, it is estimated that approximately 400 million tons of plastic waste is produced per year (Wakefield, 2022), but only 9% of plastic is recycled, 12% incinerated, and the remaining 79% is left to pollute the environment (OECD, 2018). Plastics are estimated to take anywhere from 20 to more than 450 years to degrade (WWF-Australia, 2021), leading to increasing volumes of plastic pollution. Twenty companies are estimated to produce 55% of the world's plastic waste (Charles, Kimman, & Saran, 2021). Since plastic is a major source of pollution, we will examine it in the next section.

***Recycling***

The average global recycling/composting rate is only 19%; everything else exists as waste somewhere in our environment (Trvst, 2023). Limited recycling infrastructure exists in emerging economies which adds to the waste and pollution (especially plastic waste and pollution) problem.

***Incineration***

Many cities and countries incinerate waste; some use the incineration process to produce energy (waste-to-energy) through energy recovery, gasification, pyrolysis, and anaerobic digestion methods. Incineration reduces the volume of the waste and the ash waste is then often sent to the landfill. Incineration has been criticized for producing highly toxic fly ash, air pollution and toxic emissions, and health impacts to workers and nearby residents (such as respiratory problems, infant deaths, miscarriages, and cancer).

**Pollution**

There are many forms of pollution:

- **Air**  - air pollution comes from burning fossil fuels, manufacturing, chemical production, and power generation. Air pollution causes more health problems than any other type of pollution
- **Water**  - water pollution comes from industrial activity, agriculture, mining, and urban wastewater. Bioaccumulation and biomagnification of chemicals in water are of concern for fish. **Bioaccumulation**  is the increase of a pollutant in an organism over time. **Biomagnification**  is the increase of a pollutant in a food chain. As fish are exposed to polluted water, toxic chemicals bioaccumulate in the fish and biomagnify throughout the food chain - and humans consume them.
- **Soil**  - soil pollution comes from mining, oil drilling, and agriculture.
- **Light**  - light pollution comes from exterior lighting, such as commercial properties, factories, sporting venues, and streetlights.
- **Noise**  - noise pollution comes from transportation, industrial activity, and construction.

***Environmental Justice***

Research confirms that highly polluting industries (producing air pollution, noise pollution, soil pollution, water pollution) often locate operations in low income communities, marginalized communities, communities of color, and near other disadvantaged populations. Exposure to these environmental harms results in these communities bearing a greater burden of hazardous environmental impacts and a lowered receipt of beneficial environmental impacts, a form of **environmental racism** . The movement to correct this form of racism is referred to as **environmental justice** .

***Endocrine Disruptors***

Endocrine disruptors are synthetic pollutants that interfere with the body's endocrine system. Humans are commonly exposed to BPA, phthalates, and pesticides through water, food, and non-food sources. The result of prolonged exposure or ingestion can result in several health problems.

***Plastic Pollution***

Plastic pollution is of particular concern. Plastic is made from fossil fuels. The mining and production processes create carbon emissions, thus contributing to global warming,

- Much of the plastic and other waste from Europe, the US, and Japan is exported to developing nations. China has stopped accepting garbage and plastic imports. Countries must find another method to dispose of waste.
- Approximately 1 garbage truck's worth of plastic is dumped into the ocean every minute (UNEP, 2022).
- Marine litter and plastic pollution endangers aquatic life (UNEP, 2022).

***Garbage Patch***

Much of our waste, including plastic, ends up in the environment where it often gets blown or washed into waterways and eventually carried out to sea. Ocean currents pull the plastic together resulting in 5 gyres of plastic trash accumulating in the oceans. Over time, the plastic breaks down and is consumed by marine life that mistakes the plastic for food.

***Microplastics***

Plastic also enters water and food chains through laundry. Each time clothing items are washed, small microfibers are expelled through the wastewater. Microfibers from natural material fabrics (i.e., cotton, wool, bamboo) are not problematic, but microfibers from synthetic material fabrics (i.e., polyester, nylon, polypropylene, fleece, acrylic, rayon, spandex, orlon) are also consumed by ocean life and animals. Microplastics have been found in the tiniest plankton all the way to the largest whales, in the most remote environments of the Arctic, and in our food and water. Because the microplastics enter water and food chains, it has been estimated that humans consume a credit card size volume (5 grams) of microplastics every week (WWF, 2019)!

***Bioplastic, Compostable Plastic, & Biodegradable Plastic***

Bioplastics: plastic made from plant-based materials. Sounds good. Right? Unfortunately, it has been shown that bioplastics cannot be recycled, cannot be composted, and have the same end-of-life characteristics as fossil fuel-based plastics (UNEP, 2015).

Compostable plastics must be composted in commercial composting facilities. Biodegradable plastics break down at different rates depending upon the type of plastic and the environment, often not completely breaking down at all. Many people make the mistake of thinking that compostable or biodegradable plastics will break down when thrown into the refuse pile. The fact is, they do not.

**Ecosystems, Habitats, & Biodiversity Loss**

It is estimated that nature's ecosystem services (oxygen, water, filtering harmful UV rays, bee pollination, plant roots that prevent erosion, etc.) are valued at approximately USD 150 trillion annually (Kurth et al., 2021) - that's double the global GDP. Current ecosystem decline costs over USD 5 trillion loss to the global economy each year (Kurth et al., 2021). Ecosystems regulate earth's systems, provide cultural value and habitat, and provide provisions for humans.

***Ecosystem Change***

The drivers of ecosystem change include:

- **Habitat destruction**  - human activity is destroying habitats and leading to species extinction through urban development, clearcutting, mining, deforestation, and other activities. Our human activities that cause climate change are also creating habitat destruction and causing the sixth mass extinction of planetary species, leading to a **loss of biodiversity** . Our human activity has accelerated species loss by 100 to 1000 times more than the average rate (IPBES, 2019, p. 24). This loss in biodiversity is primarily driven by: (1) changes in land and sea use; (2) direct exploitation of organisms; (3) climate change; (4) pollution and (5) invasive alien species (IPBES, 2019). Biodiversity loss damages ecosystem health which, in turn, reduces food supply, decreases habitat, and exacerbates climate change.
- **Invasive species**  - invasive species are outside species introduced by humans. The invasive species thrives and consumes resources needed by native species, thus threatening the survival of the native species and its habitat.
- **Pollution**  - all forms of pollution threaten species and their habitats.
- **Population**  - humans are the most destructive invasive species; everyplace that homo sapiens settled, species extinctions followed.
- **Overexploitation**  - humans consistently use resources faster than they can be replenished.

(https://youtu.be/NjwtneQ9cd8)

**Conservation banking**  (habitat banking, mitigation banking) is a controversial approach that allows a landowner to sell credits to protect species or habitats to companies with activities that adversely impact nature. For example, a company can destroy a parcel of land to build a shopping mall but can buy credits in another part of the world to protect threatened or endangered species elsewhere. The benefit of conservation banking is the protection of species and habitats, but the negative impact is that it allows for continued environmental degradation and converts ecosystems, habitats, and species into market commodities which leads to questions about valuation and equity. Watch this trailer for the documentary, *Banking Nature* , that explores the controversy of conservation banking.

**Tree planting**  is another controversial approach to reforestation or ecosystem and habitat protection and restoration. Some tree-planting projects use trees that grow fastest or absorb the most carbon, but the best trees to plant must be native to the ecosystem and offer a diversity of native tree species. Furthermore, a tree's ability to absorb carbon and integrate into its native environment depends on where it is planted; some planting projects interfere with water flows and other native vegetation. Tree-planting projects also cannot be guaranteed; beyond the sapling survival rate, trees die, fall, and are destroyed by fire or logging.

**Products**

Products have a significant environmental impact from the extraction of raw materials, to production, to use or consumption, to disposal or end-of-life. In other sections, you have already learned about the environmental impact of some products, such as pesticides and herbicides, plastic, and more.

***Raw Materials***

The inputs for today's products primarily come from nature: water, fossil fuels, wood, stone, metals, plants, soil, etc. Renewable resources, such as wood, natural fibers, should not be consumed at a rate faster than they can be replenished (one of the four Principles of Sustainability). Nonrenewable resources, such as granite, iron, and metal ore, are finite, that is, when we use them up, they are gone. Recycling will prolong access to nonrenewable resources but will never result in increasing supplies of the resource. As the world faces population growth, demand for nonrenewable resources is outpacing supply.

**Critical raw materials**  (CRMs) are those nonrenewable resources with economic and strategic importance to an economy, whose supply may be at risk, and that lack viable substitutes. The EU has identified a list of 30 critical raw materials that are needed in the technology, telecommunications, aerospace and aviation, electronics, medical, transportation, and defense industries.

***Packaging***

Packaging is a single-use item and humans produce a lot of single-use packaging waste - plastic, wooden pallets, crates, shipping containers. Packaging requires many resources (such as water and energy), produces carbon emissions in its manufacturing and transportation, and is discarded after a single use.

***Shipping***

Transportation emissions from shipping raw materials, parts, components, and finished products around the world are significant. From clothes made in southeast Asia, to electronics manufactured in China, to fruits and vegetables grown in South Korea, each product has associated transportation emissions when you purchase it at the store.

**Food**

***Food Production***

**Industrial agriculture**  (agribusiness) is corporate-owned large-scale farming of animals, animal products, and crops.

- **Concentrated animal feeding operations**  (**CAFO** s or factory farms) breed thousands of animals for meat, dairy, and eggs under unhealthy, unnatural, and abusive conditions. Globally, factory farms generate 60% of human-caused emissions and the livestock industry is the #1 source of methane greenhouse gasses (Clark, 2022).
- **Monoculture**  (monocropping) is a farming method of growing a single crop which destroys biodiversity and results in excessive use of chemicals to compensate for the damage, leading to water, soil, and air pollution.
- **Genetically modified organisms**  (GMOs, frankenfoods) mixes DNA of different plant species to create a new one; loses genetic diversity; no long-term impact studies of GMO consumption
- **Aquaculture**  is the use of industrial methods in fish and seafood production; also adopts monoculture practices
- **Irradition**  is the use of radiation of food to disrupt plant DNA to kill bacteria and insects; alters the flavor of the food and reduces the vitamins and nutrients
- **Deforestation**  is the clearing of forests to make way for animal and crop farmland; beef production is the #1 cause of deforestaion in the Amazon; deforestation is the 3rd largest source of emissions (Clark, 2022)

Today's industrial agriculture (Big Ag) methods of growing food have created negative social, environmental, and economic impacts:

overfishing; soil erosion; water shortages; forced labor; excessive use of fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides, and antibiotics; air, water, and soil pollution; destroyed biodiversity; health impacts on farmworkers and nearby residents; and overproduction of food.

With industrial agriculture products that go through the typical food supply chain to a supermarket, the farmer only gets 1% of the profits, the rest goes to the “middle men," such as processors, retailers, and transport companies (Jack, Hammans, Hird, Woodward, 2022). Opportunities to buy closer to the farmer can increase the farmers' percent of profits (farmers markets, co-ops, etc.)

***Food Waste***

The EU wastes more food than it imports: in 2021, the EU imported almost 138 million tons of agricultural products and wasted 153.5 tons of food (Vera, Bowman, & Mechielsen, 2022) while 33 million people did not have enough food (Eurostat, 2020). It is estimated that this costs 143 billion Euros and causes 6% of EU's greenhouse gas emissions (Vera, Bowman, & Mechielsen, 2022).

Globally, it is estimated that 14% of all food produced is lost between harvest and retail and approximately 17% of food is lost in consumption (11% by households, 5% by food services, and 2% by retail establishments); that's nearly ⅓ of all food produced in the world (United Nations, 2023).

As discarded food decays, it emits methane, a highly potent greenhouse gas (more potent than carbon dioxide) that contributes to climate change.

Food waste is a social issue, given the large number of people in the world that are food insecure, and it is an environmental issue, due to its greenhouse gas emissions and the wasted resources (energy, water, labor, land, transportation) that went into its production.

***Impacts on Human & Planetary Health***

*Humans*

- use of high fructose corn syrup and other sweeteners that lead to health problems (diabetes, high triglycerides, low HDL)
- chemicals used throughout the industrial food chain; endocrine disruptors are widely used; pesticide residue on food

*Planet*

- soils are depleted of nutrients and replaced with chemical fertilizers
- habitats are destroyed to create farmland
- approximately 70% of global freshwater is used for food production
- groundwater is depleted
- runoff of toxins gets into water supply
- nitrogen fertilizers disrupt the nitrogen cycle and contribute to climate change
- phosphorous fertilizers contaminate waterways and gets into the food systems and freshwater
- agriculture is responsible for up to 35% of global GHG emissions
- heavily dependent upon fossil fuels
- food is shipped around the world and out of season, further contributing to emissions and climate change

***Diet***

"Western” diets are heavy in meat consumption. As western foods enter diets in other parts of the world (which also contain a lot of processed foods), associated health impacts rise (obesity, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease). Plant-based diets are healthier for humans and better for the environment, as are organic foods and foods from farmers markets.

**Built Environment**

The built environment refers to all man-made structures for living, working, and recreation. This includes buildings, infrastructure (water, electricity, roads, bridges), transportation systems, etc.

Concrete, steel, and other materials to construct the built environment consume energy, produce greenhouse gas emissions, create air, water, and land pollution, and destroy habitats during the extraction, production, transportation, use, and disposal phases.

Buildings consume 40% of the world's energy and produce 37% of the world's greenhouse gases (UNEP, 2021, 2022). The building sector is the largest contributor to global GHG emissions (UNEP, n.d.). Every business requires buildings, whether for office, wholesale, retail, manufacturing, or supply chain partners. Buildings are designed for use for several decades, sometimes even centuries. Designing and retrofitting buildings for sustainability can be a cornerstone of our global sustainability strategy.
